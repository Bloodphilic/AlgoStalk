# SARIMA in Practice: Forecasting the Next Seasonal Cycle

Seasonality is easy to describe and surprisingly easy to model badly.

Suppose an operations team needs the next fourteen days of electricity demand. The business does not need a philosophical explanation of the entire historical series. It needs a forecast for the next two complete weekly cycles, produced without using information from the future.

This article builds that forecast with **SARIMA**: Seasonal AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average.

The focus is the workflow:

1. Define the forecasting horizon and preserve the time order.
2. Identify the seasonal period.
3. Difference the data when the level is not stable.
4. Fit a SARIMA model with both ordinary and seasonal terms.
5. Forecast exactly two seasonal cycles.
6. Evaluate those fourteen predictions against observations that were held out.

The data is synthetic daily electricity demand, generated by the accompanying script. It has a weekly pattern, a gradual change in level, and correlated short-term variation. The example is artificial, but the validation design is the same one I would want to see in a real forecasting project.

## The business question comes first

The question is:

> Given demand up to today, how accurately can we forecast the next fourteen days?

Fourteen days is two complete weekly cycles. It tests whether the model can preserve the seasonal pattern beyond the immediately following week. The final fourteen observations are kept untouched until evaluation. They are not used to choose parameters or fit the model.

![Time series and forecast boundary](visuals/step_1_series_split.png)

*Figure 1. The training data ends before the final two weekly cycles. The shaded region is the fourteen-day evaluation horizon that the model must forecast without seeing the actual values.*

This distinction matters. A model can fit historical data beautifully and still fail at the boundary where forecasting begins.

## Step 1: identify the seasonal period

For daily data, a weekly seasonal period is usually represented by `m = 7`. That does not mean every daily series is seasonal with period seven. It means we have a hypothesis to test: does demand at a given weekday resemble demand at the same weekday in the previous week? The forecast horizon can still be fourteen days; `m=7` describes the repeating cycle, not the number of observations being forecast.

Two simple checks are useful:

- Compare the average demand by weekday.
- Inspect autocorrelation at lag 7 and nearby seasonal lags.

The weekday profile below is calculated using training data only. The repeated shape is strong enough to justify a weekly seasonal model, but the weekday averages are not themselves a forecast. They ignore the changing level and the dependence between consecutive observations.

![Weekly seasonal pattern](visuals/step_2_seasonal_period.png)

*Figure 2. The training data has a repeatable weekly pattern. The seasonal period used by SARIMA is therefore seven days.*

## What SARIMA means

SARIMA extends ARIMA by adding a second set of terms at the seasonal lag.

It is commonly written as:

```text
SARIMA(p, d, q)(P, D, Q, m)
```

The terms have different jobs:

- `p`: ordinary autoregressive terms, using recent observations.
- `d`: ordinary differencing, used to remove a persistent level or trend.
- `q`: ordinary moving-average terms, using recent forecast errors.
- `P`: seasonal autoregressive terms, using observations such as `t - 7`.
- `D`: seasonal differencing, comparing an observation with the same season in the previous cycle.
- `Q`: seasonal moving-average terms, using errors from previous seasonal cycles.
- `m`: the seasonal period; here, seven days.

For this experiment, the fitted model is:

```text
SARIMA(1, 1, 1)(1, 1, 1, 7)
```

This is not a claim that these orders are universally correct. They are a compact, interpretable starting specification for a daily series with weekly repetition and a changing level.

## Step 2: make the seasonal structure easier to model

The `I` in SARIMA means **integrated**. In practice, it means differencing the series before modelling it, then accumulating the predicted changes to return to the original scale.

Ordinary differencing is:

```text
Δy(t) = y(t) - y(t-1)
```

Seasonal differencing with a weekly period is:

```text
Δ7y(t) = y(t) - y(t-7)
```

The first comparison removes much of the local level movement. The second asks whether today's demand is different from the demand on the same weekday last week. SARIMA can apply both with `d=1` and `D=1`.

The transformations are not cosmetic. They determine what the AR and MA terms are asked to model. If we leave a strong changing level in the data, the model may interpret non-stationary movement as persistence.

![Ordinary and seasonal differencing](visuals/step_3_differencing.png)

*Figure 3. Differencing changes the modelling target from the absolute demand level to changes between nearby and same-season observations.*

Differencing has a cost: the transformed series is less intuitive, and the first observations lost to the lags cannot be used directly. It should be used to create a more stable modelling problem, not applied automatically because a library accepts a `d` parameter.

## Step 3: fit the model on the training window

The model is fitted only on the observations before the final fourteen days:

```python
SARIMAX(
    train,
    order=(1, 1, 1),
    seasonal_order=(1, 1, 1, 7),
)
```

The ordinary AR term allows recent demand changes to influence the next change. The ordinary MA term accounts for recent forecast errors. The seasonal AR term connects the current value to the previous weekly cycle, while the seasonal MA term carries information from previous seasonal errors.

Those terms are not four independent explanations that can be read off a chart. They interact. Increasing model complexity can improve in-sample fit while making the model less stable or less useful at the forecast boundary.

After fitting, residuals are inspected. A good residual diagnostic is not “the residual is small everywhere.” It is whether the residuals still show a systematic pattern that the model should have captured.

![SARIMA fit and residual diagnostics](visuals/step_4_model_diagnostics.png)

*Figure 4. The fitted values should follow the training series without simply memorising every fluctuation. The residual autocorrelation is a check for structure left behind by the model.*

## Step 4: forecast two complete seasonal cycles

The forecast horizon is fourteen observations, so the model produces two full weekly cycles. The confidence interval reflects uncertainty in the model and the future innovations; it is not a guarantee that the actual value will fall inside it.

The forecast is generated after the model has seen the complete training window, but before it sees any of the fourteen held-out demand values.

![Seven-day SARIMA forecast](visuals/step_5_forecast.png)

*Figure 5. The forecast begins at the boundary and covers two complete seasonal cycles. The held-out observations are shown only for evaluation.*

The useful visual question is not whether every point lands exactly on the line. It is whether the model preserves the weekly timing, tracks the changing level, and keeps its errors within a plausible range.

## Step 5: evaluate the forecast, not the fit

For the fourteen held-out observations, the script reports:

```text
MAE  = mean(abs(actual - forecast))
RMSE = sqrt(mean((actual - forecast)^2))
```

MAE is in demand units and is easy to explain operationally. RMSE penalises a large miss more heavily, which can matter if one bad day is much more costly than several moderate errors.

The measured values are written by the script when it runs. They should be treated as results of this fixed synthetic seed, not as evidence that SARIMA will beat every alternative. On real data, a seasonal naive baseline—“next Monday equals last Monday”—is essential. A complicated model that cannot beat that baseline is difficult to justify.

## What the experiment does and does not prove

This experiment demonstrates a disciplined SARIMA workflow. It does not prove that `(1,1,1)(1,1,1,7)` is the best possible specification, or that SARIMA is the right model for every seasonal series.

Important extensions would include:

- rolling-origin backtesting over many forecast windows;
- comparison with a seasonal-naive baseline;
- parameter search guided by out-of-sample error, not AIC alone;
- exogenous variables such as temperature, holidays, or outages;
- checking whether the seasonal period changes over time.

SARIMA is most useful when its assumptions are visible and testable. It gives us a structured way to express persistence, differencing, and seasonal memory. It does not remove the need to understand the data-generating process.

## Final takeaway

The important result is not the model name. It is the sequence of decisions:

1. Reserve the final two seasonal cycles before fitting.
2. Establish the seasonal period from the data and the operating context.
3. Difference only as much as needed to create a stable target.
4. Use ordinary and seasonal AR/MA terms to represent short-term and seasonal memory.
5. Judge the model on a genuinely unseen forecast horizon.

That is how SARIMA becomes more than a line of code: each parameter corresponds to a time-series behaviour, and each visual gives us a way to question whether that behaviour is present.
